Replicating Brownlees, Engle & Kelly (2011): A Practical Guide to Volatility Forecasting through Calm and Storm¶

The short version. Brownlees, Engle and Kelly took the GARCH family of volatility models and asked practical questions:

  • Which model forecasts best?
  • How far ahead does that hold?
  • Did the models break down in the autumn of 2008?

I rebuilt their S&P 500 forecasting exercise from free data, using the same five models and the same estimation scheme (all data since 1990, re-estimated every week), and scored the forecasts with the same loss function.

The main table comes back very close:

  • Losses. My 25 average losses (five models × five horizons) are within 0.007 of the paper's on average.
  • Ranking. The one-day ranking of the five models is identical: TARCH first, plain GARCH last.

The crisis results reproduce too. One-day forecasts were about as good in fall 2008 as in any other period, while one-month forecasts were far worse. The paper's historical and simulated probabilities of a loss that bad come back to within a few percentage points.

One part doesn't reproduce. The paper's loss levels for the panels of sector and country ETFs are about 2.2. Mine are about 1.25, which is roughly the lowest a correct model can score. I show why my numbers are plausible and list what I tested.

1. How the forecasts are scored¶

A volatility forecast can't be compared with "true" volatility, because nobody observes it. The paper compares each variance forecast $h$ with a noisy stand-in, the squared return $r^2$, using the QL loss:

$$\text{QL}(r^2, h) = \frac{r^2}{h} - \log\frac{r^2}{h} - 1 .$$

QL is zero when $r^2 = h$, and it depends only on the ratio of the two. That has two useful consequences:

  • A day in 2008 and a calm day in 2005 count equally.
  • With an unbiased proxy, ranking models by average QL gives the same order as ranking them against the true variance (Patton 2011).

Section 3.5 of the paper turns average losses into something easier to picture. Suppose true daily volatility is 1.46%. Which forecast, too low or too high, would produce a given average loss? I solve that equation for the two losses the paper quotes (0.247 for GARCH and 0.243 for TARCH, using the realized-volatility proxy).

def ql(proxy, forecast):
    x = proxy / forecast
    return x - np.log(x) - 1

# the paper's economic translation (Section 3.5): which volatility forecasts x give a QL loss equal to the average loss?
TRUE_VOL = 0.0146
def implied_forecasts(avg_loss, s=TRUE_VOL):
    f = lambda x: s ** 2 / x ** 2 - np.log(s ** 2 / x ** 2) - 1 - avg_loss
    return brentq(f, 1e-4, s), brentq(f, s, 1.0)        # an underestimate and an overestimate

under_g, over_g = implied_forecasts(0.247)               # GARCH, realized-volatility proxy (the paper's numbers)
under_t, over_t = implied_forecasts(0.243)               # TARCH
print(f"GARCH: under {under_g:.4f}, over {over_g:.4f} (paper 0.0105, 0.0222)")
print(f"TARCH: under {under_t:.4f}, over {over_t:.4f} (paper 0.0107, 0.0217)")
red = lambda xt, xg: 1 - (xt - TRUE_VOL) / (xg - TRUE_VOL)
print(f"volatility error reduction, TARCH vs GARCH: {red(under_t, under_g):.1%} (under), {red(over_t, over_g):.1%} (over)  [paper 4.3% to 7.7%]")
var_red = lambda xt, xg: 1 - (norm.ppf(0.01, 0, xt) - norm.ppf(0.01, 0, TRUE_VOL)) / (norm.ppf(0.01, 0, xg) - norm.ppf(0.01, 0, TRUE_VOL))
print(f"1% VaR error reduction: {var_red(under_t, under_g):.1%}, {var_red(over_t, over_g):.1%}")

The implied forecasts match the paper's: GARCH's loss corresponds to a forecast of 1.07% (too low) or 2.17% (too high), and the paper prints 1.05% and 2.22%. For TARCH both pairs are 1.07% and 2.17%. The small GARCH difference is just rounding in the losses the paper prints.

The next sentence of the paper doesn't follow from these numbers, though. The paper says TARCH cuts the forecast error by 4.3% to 7.7% compared with GARCH. With losses of 0.247 and 0.243, the reduction is only 0.6% (under) and 1.1% (over). Even the paper's own rounded forecasts give about 5% and 7%. The percentages were probably computed from unrounded or different losses. Either way, the qualitative point stands: a small difference in average QL is a small difference in volatility, and therefore in VaR.

2. Data¶

The paper uses daily split- and dividend-adjusted S&P 500 returns from Datastream. The free equivalent is Yahoo's S&P 500 total return index (^SP500TR), which reinvests dividends. Returns are log differences in percent.

One issue needs fixing first. QL takes the log of $r^2$, so a day with a return of exactly zero gives an infinite loss. The total return index has a handful of days where its value didn't change. On those days, which are stale prints rather than genuinely flat markets, I rebuild the return as the price index's move plus that month's average daily dividend accrual.

3. The five models and the base strategy¶

All five models describe tomorrow's variance $h_{t+1}$ as a function of today's return and today's variance:

Model Variance equation What it adds
GARCH(1,1) $h_{t+1} = \omega + \alpha r_t^2 + \beta h_t$ Volatility clustering
TARCH $h_{t+1} = \omega + (\alpha + \gamma\,1_{r_t<0})\, r_t^2 + \beta h_t$ Falls raise volatility more than rises
EGARCH $\log h_{t+1} = \omega + \alpha(\lvert z_t\rvert - E\lvert z_t\rvert) + \gamma z_t + \beta \log h_t$ Asymmetry, and variance can't go negative
APARCH $h_{t+1}^{\delta/2} = \omega + \alpha(\lvert r_t\rvert - \gamma r_t)^\delta + \beta h_t^{\delta/2}$ Estimates the power as well
NGARCH $h_{t+1} = \omega + \alpha (r_t + \gamma)^2 + \beta h_t$ Asymmetry by shifting the news term

The paper's base strategy:

  • Estimation: every model is estimated by Gaussian maximum likelihood on all returns from January 1990 up to the end of the previous week.
  • Re-estimation: parameters are re-estimated once a week.
  • Forecasts: at the end of each day, the model forecasts the variance 1, 5, 10, 15 and 22 trading days ahead, using the current week's parameters.

For GARCH and TARCH, multi-step forecasts have a closed form. EGARCH and APARCH have none, so their forecasts are averaged over 1,000 simulated paths. The arch package doesn't include NGARCH, so I wrote its likelihood out by hand. The variance recursion is a linear filter, which keeps the hundreds of weekly fits fast.

SPECS = {"GARCH": dict(vol="GARCH", p=1, o=0, q=1), "TARCH": dict(vol="GARCH", p=1, o=1, q=1),
         "EGARCH": dict(vol="EGARCH", p=1, o=1, q=1), "APARCH": dict(vol="APARCH", p=1, o=1, q=1)}


def ngarch_path(params, x, h0):
    # h_{t+1} = w + a (r_t + g)^2 + b h_t ; a linear filter in h, so it runs at C speed
    w, a, b, g = params
    h = np.empty(len(x) + 1)
    h[0] = h0
    h[1:] = lfilter([1.0], [1.0, -b], w + a * (x + g) ** 2, zi=[b * h0])[0]
    return h


def ngarch_fit(x, start=None):
    h0 = x.var()
    def nll(p):
        w, a, b, g = p
        if w <= 0 or a < 0 or b < 0 or a + b >= 0.9999:
            return 1e10
        h = ngarch_path(p, x, h0)[:-1]
        return 0.5 * np.sum(np.log(h) + x ** 2 / h)
    p0 = start if start is not None else np.array([0.02, 0.06, 0.9, -0.5])
    return minimize(nll, p0, method="Nelder-Mead", options={"maxiter": 4000, "xatol": 1e-6, "fatol": 1e-6}).x, h0


def forecast_paths(r, model, test_start, test_end, first_obs=None, dist="normal", cache=None):
    # Base strategy: expanding window from the first observation, parameters re-estimated weekly,
    # forecasts of the variance 1, 5, 10, 15 and 22 days ahead made at the end of every day.
    if cache and Path(cache).exists():
        return pd.read_csv(cache, index_col=0, parse_dates=True)
    r = r if first_obs is None else r.loc[first_obs:]
    pos = {d: i for i, d in enumerate(r.index)}
    first = pos[r.loc[test_start:].index[0]]
    stop = pos[r.loc[:test_end].index[-1]]
    starts = r.index[first - 1:stop + 1].to_series().groupby(r.index[first - 1:stop + 1].to_period("W")).first()
    out = {h: np.full(len(r), np.nan) for h in HORIZONS}
    params = None
    for ws in starts:
        i0 = pos[ws]
        end = min(i0 + 5, stop + 1)
        origins = range(i0 - 1, end)
        if model == "NGARCH":
            params, h0 = ngarch_fit(r.values[:i0], params)
            h = ngarch_path(params, r.values[:end], h0)
            w, a, b, g = params
            var = []
            for o in origins:
                cur, row = h[o + 1], []
                for _ in range(22):
                    row.append(cur); cur = w + a * g ** 2 + (a + b) * cur
                var.append(row)
            var = np.array(var)
        else:
            res = arch_model(r.iloc[:i0], mean="Zero", dist=dist, **SPECS[model]).fit(disp="off", starting_values=params, show_warning=False)
            params = res.params.values
            fixed = arch_model(r.iloc[:end], mean="Zero", dist=dist, **SPECS[model]).fix(params)
            if model in ("GARCH", "TARCH"):
                var = fixed.forecast(horizon=22, start=i0 - 1, reindex=False).variance.values
            else:
                var = fixed.forecast(horizon=22, start=i0 - 1, reindex=False, method="simulation", simulations=1000).variance.values
        for o, row in zip(origins, var):
            for h in HORIZONS:
                if o + h <= stop:
                    out[h][o + h] = row[h - 1]
    df = pd.DataFrame({f"h{h}": out[h] for h in HORIZONS}, index=r.index).loc[test_start:test_end]
    if cache:
        df.to_csv(cache)
    return df


def avg_ql(r, paths, start=None, end=None, drop_zero=True, dating="origin"):
    # dating="origin": a forecast counts towards a window when it was made inside the window and its target day is inside it too
    # dating="target": it counts whenever its target day is inside the window
    rr = r.reindex(paths.index)
    keep = rr != 0 if drop_zero else rr.notna()
    out = {}
    for h, lab in zip(HORIZONS, HLABEL):
        f = paths.loc[keep, f"h{h}"].loc[start:end].dropna()
        if dating == "origin":
            made = r.index[r.index.get_indexer(f.index) - h]
            f = f[made >= pd.Timestamp(start or paths.index[0])]
        out[lab] = ql(rr[f.index] ** 2, f).mean()
    return pd.Series(out)

This is a close match.

  • Average gap: across the 25 cells it is 0.007. Mine are a touch lower almost everywhere: the mean gap is −0.006, and only three cells are higher.
  • Ranking: at one day it is exactly the paper's: TARCH (1.405), APARCH, EGARCH, NGARCH, GARCH (1.451).
  • Largest gap: EGARCH at one month, 0.027. EGARCH's multi-step forecasts are simulated, and their exact form depends on implementation details that the paper doesn't spell out.

The pattern is the paper's main result:

  • The asymmetric models win at short horizons. At one day, TARCH's loss is 0.046 below GARCH's: when the market falls, the asymmetric models raise next-day volatility more, and that's right more often than not.
  • The advantage narrows with the horizon. By one month the five models are within 0.05 of each other. Today's sign tells you little about volatility a month away.
  • Losses rise with the horizon. For every model they go from about 1.41–1.45 at one day to 1.62–1.66 at one month, because forecasting further ahead is harder.

Why mine run slightly lower: Yahoo's total return index and Datastream's series aren't identical day by day. A loss that averages $-\log(r^2/h)$ is sensitive to small returns, so tiny data differences on quiet days move the average by a few thousandths.

5. Table 3: fall 2008¶

The paper's main question is whether the models failed in the crisis. Table 3 compares TARCH's losses over 2001–2008 with those from September to December 2008.

One detail isn't stated in the paper: is a multi-step forecast counted in the crisis window by the date it was made or by the day it targets? I compute both. Under the first reading, a forecast counts when it was made inside the window and targets a day inside the window. The second counts every forecast whose target day falls inside the window.

Dating by when forecasts were made gives the paper's pattern and close numbers:

  • Short horizons barely change: 1.41 at one day and 1.54 at one week, against 1.41 and 1.43 over the full sample.
  • Long horizons get much worse: at one month the crisis loss is 2.83, against the paper's 2.875.

Dating by target day gives 3.70 at one month, too high: it includes forecasts made in calm August for days in September. I use the origin dating for the out-of-sample tables. The one cell where it fits worse is two weeks (1.84 against the paper's 1.99).

The paper's interpretation reproduces:

  • One-day forecasts held up. The crisis was violent but each day's volatility was well predicted by the previous day's, because the models react fast to large moves.
  • One-month forecasts failed. No GARCH model predicted that volatility would stay that high for that long. All five models show the same split, and TARCH is the best of them at every horizon.

The chart shows the same thing. The forecast line follows the jump in absolute returns within days, but it started from a very low level in mid-2008.

def in_sample_paths(r, dist):
    res = arch_model(r, mean="Zero", vol="GARCH", p=1, o=1, q=1, dist=dist).fit(disp="off", show_warning=False)
    w, a, g, b = res.params[["omega", "alpha[1]", "gamma[1]", "beta[1]"]].values
    h1 = res.conditional_volatility.values ** 2                     # one-step variance for each day
    out = {1: h1}
    rho = a + g / 2 + b
    for h in HORIZONS[1:]:
        f = np.full(len(r), np.nan)
        # forecast of day t made at t-h: iterate the expected-variance recursion forward from the one-step value at t-h+1
        base = h1[:len(r) - h + 1]
        cur = base.copy()
        for _ in range(h - 1):
            cur = w + rho * cur
        f[h - 1:] = cur
        out[h] = f
    return pd.DataFrame({f"h{h}": out[h] for h in HORIZONS}, index=r.index), res.params

8. Table 6: how likely was a fall like 2008?¶

The paper answers this in two ways:

  • Historically: how often, since 1926, has an 85-day window had average losses at least as large as fall 2008's?
  • By simulation: simulate 82-year histories from the fitted TARCH-t model, re-estimate the model on each, and count how often the last four months produce losses that large. This builds estimation error into the answer.

The paper uses 5,000 simulations, and so do I. The crisis losses are the Student t ones from Table 5, the same model the simulations use.

# Monte Carlo under a TARCH-t world: simulate 82 years, re-estimate, compute in-sample losses in the last four months
sim_cache = DATA / "table6_simulations.csv"
N_SIM = 5000                                   # as in the paper
if sim_cache.exists():
    sims = pd.read_csv(sim_cache)
else:
    pt = fitted["t"]
    w, a, g, b, nu = pt[["omega", "alpha[1]", "gamma[1]", "beta[1]", "nu"]].values
    T = len(mkt)
    rng = np.random.default_rng(2008)
    out = []
    t0 = time.time()
    for s in range(N_SIM):
        z = rng.standard_t(nu, T) * np.sqrt((nu - 2) / nu)
        r = np.empty(T); h = w / (1 - a - g / 2 - b)
        for t in range(T):
            r[t] = np.sqrt(h) * z[t]
            h = w + (a + g * (r[t] < 0)) * r[t] ** 2 + b * h

10. Do the numbers make sense?¶

  • The losses sit where theory says they should. A perfect forecast of normal returns scores 1.27 on average. Real returns have fatter tails and the models aren't perfect, so S&P losses of 1.40–1.66 are what you'd expect. The crisis numbers are higher, as they should be.
  • The model ranking makes economic sense. The leverage effect (falls raise volatility more than rises) is one of the most reliable facts about equity returns, so the asymmetric models should win, and their edge should fade with the horizon.
  • The fitted parameters look right. TARCH's persistence ($\alpha + \gamma/2 + \beta$) is 0.990 to 0.992 at every date in the cell above. The asymmetry term $\gamma$ (0.10 to 0.12) is far larger than $\alpha$ (0.003 to 0.013): almost all of the reaction to news comes from down days, as is usual for US equities.
  • Every gap is explained or flagged. Table 2 differs by about as much as a single rebuilt day can move it. The fall-2008 window depends on how forecasts are dated, and I show both. Table 4's levels are lower than a correct model can explain, and I report that rather than adjust anything to match.

11. Is the procedure right?¶

  • No look-ahead. Each week's parameters use only returns up to the previous Friday, and each forecast uses only returns up to its own day.
  • Same models, likelihood and update scheme as the paper's base strategy. NGARCH is coded by hand with the same Gaussian likelihood as the others; each week's fit starts from the previous week's estimates.
  • The scoring is exactly the paper's QL loss. Zero-return days are rebuilt for the S&P (four days in 36 years) and dropped for the panels, with the counts printed.
  • The paper's values are typed in only as comparison targets. Nothing in the replication is set from them. The window-dating choice is shown both ways, so it can be judged.

Wrapping up¶

The core of the paper reproduces from free data:

  • The ranking. The five-model, five-horizon loss table comes back to within 0.007 on average, with the same one-day order.
  • The asymmetry pattern. Asymmetric models win at short horizons, and the advantage fades by a month.
  • The crisis finding. One-day forecasts in fall 2008 were about normal, one-month forecasts were extremely poor, and the historical and simulated odds of that are close to the paper's.

The 2009–2026 extension and the Covid crash say the same thing again.

What doesn't reproduce is the level of the losses for the ETF panels in Table 4. My values are at the floor a correct model should reach, and none of the alternatives I tried gets near the paper's figures. I've left that as an open difference. The Section 3.5 percentages also don't follow from the paper's own losses, which I've noted.

For risk management, the lesson is the one the authors draw. GARCH models are excellent at telling you tomorrow's risk and poor at telling you next month's in a regime change. Short-horizon VaR from these models is reliable, and long-horizon risk needs more than a GARCH forecast.

Next steps:

  • Add a realized-volatility proxy (for example from 5-minute index data) to reproduce the "rv" half of Tables 2 and 3.
  • Test the alternative estimation strategies (shorter windows, daily updates, Student t errors) with the Diebold–Mariano test the paper uses.
  • Try models built for long horizons, such as component GARCH or HAR on realized volatility, to see whether they close the one-month gap in crises.
