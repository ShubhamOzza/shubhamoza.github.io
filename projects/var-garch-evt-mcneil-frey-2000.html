Replicating McNeil & Frey (2000): GARCH plus extreme value theory for Value-at-Risk¶

The short version. McNeil and Frey proposed a two-step way to forecast tomorrow's Value-at-Risk:

  1. A GARCH model captures how volatility changes from day to day.
  2. Extreme value theory models the fat tail of what's left over.

They backtested it against three alternatives on five long return series. I re-ran their backtest on the two series that are freely available for the same dates: the S&P 500 from 1960 to 1993 and the dollar-sterling rate from 1980 to 1996. That means refitting four models every single day, about 10,700 times.

  • Verdicts. In 23 of the 24 cells of their Table 2 (2 series × 3 confidence levels × 4 methods), I reach the same accept/reject verdict. The one exception sits on the 5% line: 49 violations against the paper's 50.
  • Counts. All 24 violation counts are within two binomial standard deviations of the paper's.
  • The headline. As in the paper, conditional EVT is never rejected, and a GARCH model with normal errors fails badly at the 99% and 99.5% levels.

I then ran the same backtest out of sample, from 1993 to September 2026, through 2008 and 2020. The ranking holds, though the 99.5% S&P quantile is now borderline even for the best method.

2. The method on one day: the Friday before Black Monday¶

McNeil and Frey's method has two stages:

  1. Filter the volatility. Fit an AR(1)-GARCH(1,1) model to the last 1,000 losses by pseudo-maximum likelihood: the likelihood assumes normal errors, but the method doesn't actually rely on normality. This gives tomorrow's expected loss μ and volatility σ, and a set of standardised residuals z, which should be independent and identically distributed if the model is right.
  2. Model the tail of z. Take the 100 largest residuals, fit a generalised Pareto distribution (GPD) to how far they exceed the 101st, and read off the quantile $z_q$ from the paper's Equation 10.

The VaR forecast is then $x_q = \mu + \sigma z_q$. The three benchmarks use the same structure:

  • Conditional normal uses the same GARCH fit but takes $z_q$ from the normal distribution.
  • Conditional t fits a separate GARCH model with Student-t errors by full maximum likelihood.
  • Unconditional EVT skips the GARCH step and fits the GPD straight to the raw losses.

Here's the whole thing on the 1,000 days up to Friday 16 October 1987.

def fit_garch(window, dist):
    # AR(1)-GARCH(1,1) with no constant in the mean, as in Equations 4-5 of the paper
    model = ARX(window, lags=1, constant=False, volatility=GARCH(1, 0, 1), distribution=dist)
    return model.fit(disp="off", show_warning=False)


def gpd_tail_quantile(sample, qs=QS, k=K_TAIL):
    # Equation 10: fit a GPD to the k largest excesses over the (k+1)th largest value
    ordered = np.sort(sample)[::-1]
    u = ordered[k]
    xi, _, beta = genpareto.fit(ordered[:k] - u, floc=0)
    n = len(sample)
    return {q: u + beta / xi * (((1 - q) / (k / n)) ** (-xi) - 1) for q in qs}, (u, xi, beta)

  • The GARCH fit is typical of daily equity data. α₁ + β = 0.997, so volatility is extremely persistent. The AR(1) coefficient of 0.10 picks up the slight day-to-day momentum in the 1980s index.
  • The QQ-plot shows why normal errors are wrong. The residuals' upper tail (large losses) bends away from the normal line.
  • The tail plot shows what the GPD does instead. It follows the empirical tail, while the normal tail drops away far too fast.
  • This changes the forecasts in an interesting way. At 95% the EVT VaR is actually lower than the normal one (2.82% against 3.04%). At 99% and 99.5% it's higher (4.35% against 4.07%, and 5.12% against 4.45%). Fat-tailed distributions have more mass in the far tail and less in the shoulder, so they cross the normal distribution, and that's exactly what the residuals show.
  • None of this would have saved you on Black Monday. The loss was 22.9%, more than five times the 99% VaR. One day's model can't anticipate a move like that; what matters is how often each method is violated over thousands of days, and how quickly it adapts afterwards.

3. The backtest: refitting every day¶

For every day in the test period, all four models are refitted on the previous 1,000 losses, and the next day's 95%, 99% and 99.5% quantiles are forecast. A violation happens when the actual loss exceeds the forecast.

If a method is correct, violations at level q happen independently with probability 1 − q, so their count follows a binomial distribution. The paper uses a two-sided binomial test, where a p-value of 0.05 or below means the method has too many or too few violations to be believed.

This is about 21,000 model fits for the two series together. Each day's fit starts from the previous day's estimates, which is much faster, and the results are saved to data/ so the notebook can be re-read without refitting.

def backtest(x, label):
    # Refit everything every day on the last 1,000 losses and forecast the next day's quantiles.
    cache = DATA / f"backtest_{label}.csv"
    if cache.exists():
        return pd.read_csv(cache, index_col=0, parse_dates=True)
    vals, rows = x.values, []
    start_n = start_t = None
    t0 = time.time()
    for t in range(N_WIN, len(vals)):
        win = vals[t - N_WIN:t]
        rn = ARX(win, lags=1, constant=False, volatility=GARCH(1, 0, 1), distribution=Normal()) \
            .fit(disp="off", starting_values=start_n, show_warning=False)
        start_n = rn.params.values
        phi, w, a, b = start_n
        mu = phi * win[-1]
        sig = np.sqrt(w + a * rn.resid[-1] ** 2 + b * rn.conditional_volatility[-1] ** 2)
        zq, _ = gpd_tail_quantile(rn.std_resid[~np.isnan(rn.std_resid)])

        rt = ARX(win, lags=1, constant=False, volatility=GARCH(1, 0, 1), distribution=StudentsT()) \
            .fit(disp="off", starting_values=start_t, show_warning=False)
        start_t = rt.params.values
        phi_t, w_t, a_t, b_t, nu = start_t
        mu_t = phi_t * win[-1]
        sig_t = np.sqrt(w_t + a_t * rt.resid[-1] ** 2 + b_t * rt.conditional_volatility[-1] ** 2)

        uq, _ = gpd_tail_quantile(win)
        row = {"date": x.index[t], "loss": vals[t], "sigma": sig}
        for q in QS:
            row[f"evt_{q}"] = mu + sig * zq[q]
            row[f"norm_{q}"] = mu + sig * norm.ppf(q)
            row[f"t_{q}"] = mu_t + sig_t * student_t.ppf(q, nu) * np.sqrt((nu - 2) / nu)
            row[f"uevt_{q}"] = uq[q]
        rows.append(row)
    out = pd.DataFrame(rows).set_index("date")
    out.to_csv(cache)
    print(f"{label}: {len(out)} days in {time.time() - t0:.0f}s")
    return out

def violations(bt, method, q):
    hits = (bt.loss > bt[f"{method}_{q}"]).astype(int)
    return hits.sum(), binomtest(hits.sum(), len(hits), 1 - q).pvalue, hits

The two tables tell the same story:

  • Conditional EVT is never rejected. Its counts are close to expected everywhere (for example 368 against 371, and 72 against 74 on the S&P 500), as in the paper.
  • Conditional normal fails at the high quantiles. It's rejected at 99% and 99.5% on both series. The S&P 500 at 99.5% has 58 violations against 37 expected (the paper: 63), and $/£ at 99.5% has 41 against 16 (the paper: also 41). At 95% it's fine, because the shoulder of the distribution is close to normal.
  • Conditional t and unconditional EVT sit in between, as in the paper. Unconditional EVT has about the right number of violations but, as Section 5 shows, it gets them at the wrong times.

The single verdict that differs is unconditional EVT at 99.5% on the S&P 500. I get 49 violations (p = 0.06) and the paper 50 (p = 0.04). One violation either way crosses the 5% line, so it's a coin-flip rather than a disagreement.

Measured in binomial standard deviations, every one of the 24 counts is within 2 of the paper's, and 19 are within 1. For conditional EVT and unconditional EVT, the average gap is under a tenth of a standard deviation, so for the paper's own method the match is very close.

One pattern is worth reporting. On the S&P 500, my conditional normal and conditional t models have fewer violations than the paper's at all three levels: for example 348 against 384 for the normal model at 95%, and 371 against 404 for the t model. Within a model the three levels are strongly linked, so this is really one piece of evidence, and each gap is under two standard deviations. Still, I checked the most obvious implementation difference. To save time, my daily fits start from the previous day's estimates, and the optimiser could settle somewhere different from a fresh start. Re-running the whole S&P 500 backtest with every fit started from scratch gave exactly the same counts (348, 95 and 58 for the normal model; 371, 74 and 42 for t). So it isn't the optimiser's starting point.

The remaining candidates are the data and the fitting software. The paper's S&P 500 series came from a different vendor, and its test set is 13 days shorter. Its GARCH fits were done in 1999 software with its own conventions for starting values of the variance recursion. Both affect the normal and t models through σ alone, while the EVT method re-estimates the tail from the residuals and partly absorbs a small error in σ. That would explain why the EVT cells match closely and the normal and t cells less so. The paper doesn't give enough detail to test this further, so I report it rather than adjust anything. None of it changes a single conclusion.

The difference between the two EVT approaches is clearest here.

  • The conditional VaR (solid line) adapts. Its 99% level was 4.35% for Black Monday and jumped to 26.1% for the next day, because GARCH volatility responds immediately to a 22.9% loss. It then decayed back to about 2.9% by March 1988 as the market calmed. From October to December 1987 it was violated on 5 days.
  • The unconditional VaR (dashed line) barely moves. It's a quantile of the last 1,000 days pooled together, so one extreme week hardly shifts it. On 19 October 1987 it stood at just 2.33%, and it was violated on four trading days in a row (14 to 19 October) and on 10 days between October and December 1987.

That's the paper's main argument in one picture. Fat tails matter, but so does volatility that changes from day to day, and only conditional EVT handles both.

5. Beyond the paper's Table 2: are the violations independent?¶

The binomial test only counts violations. A method could have the right number of violations and still be badly wrong, if they arrive in clusters: several bad days in a row are far more dangerous than the same number spread over years. The paper mentions this informally for unconditional EVT. Christoffersen's (1998) independence test makes it formal: it asks whether a violation today makes one tomorrow more likely. The test was published after McNeil and Frey wrote the paper, so this is an extension.

In [9]:
def christoffersen(hits):
    # likelihood-ratio test that a violation today doesn't make one tomorrow more likely (Christoffersen 1998)
    h = np.asarray(hits)
    n00 = np.sum((h[:-1] == 0) & (h[1:] == 0)); n01 = np.sum((h[:-1] == 0) & (h[1:] == 1))
    n10 = np.sum((h[:-1] == 1) & (h[1:] == 0)); n11 = np.sum((h[:-1] == 1) & (h[1:] == 1))
    p01, p11 = n01 / (n00 + n01), (n11 / (n10 + n11) if n10 + n11 else 0.0)
    p = (n01 + n11) / (n00 + n01 + n10 + n11)
    lr = -2 * (((n00 + n10) * np.log(1 - p) + (n01 + n11) * np.log(p))
               - (n00 * np.log(1 - p01) + n01 * np.log(p01) + n10 * np.log(1 - p11) + (n11 * np.log(p11) if n11 else 0)))
    return n11, chi2.sf(lr, 1)

6. Out of sample: 1993 to 2026¶

The paper's results end in the mid-1990s. The obvious question is whether the ranking survives the 33 years since, which include the dot-com bust, 2008, the 2020 Covid crash and the 2022 sterling crisis. I re-ran exactly the same procedure: the same models, the same 1,000-day window, k = 100, and no retuning. It starts with enough history to make the first forecast on 1 July 1993 for the S&P 500 and 3 June 1996 for $/£. That's about 16,300 more forecast days.

  • The ranking holds. Conditional EVT is the only method that passes at every level on the S&P 500 (432, 98 and 55 violations against 418, 84 and 42 expected). It's only just there at 99.5% (p = 0.05, which by the paper's rule counts as a rejection), so the far tail of the last 30 years has been a little harsher than the model expected. On $/£ it passes everywhere.
  • Conditional normal fails at 99% and 99.5% on both series. At 99% on the S&P 500 it has 180 violations against 84 expected, more than twice what it should.
  • Conditional t does worse out of sample than in the paper's period. It's rejected at every level on the S&P 500. A likely reason is that its symmetric t distribution has to fit both tails at once. McNeil and Frey point out that their residuals were asymmetric, with a heavier loss tail than gain tail, and a symmetric distribution can't capture that. The EVT method only models the loss tail, so it doesn't have this problem.
  • Unconditional EVT is rejected on the S&P 500 but passes on $/£. Equity volatility swings between calm and crisis much more than exchange-rate volatility does, and a method that ignores volatility can't keep up.

The rolling violation rates show when things go wrong. Every method spikes in late 2008 and early 2020. In 2008, conditional EVT had 8 violations of its 99% VaR in 253 days (2.5 expected), against 12 for the normal model and 27 for unconditional EVT. No daily model keeps up with the first days of a crash. The difference is how many days each one takes to catch up.

7. Do the numbers make sense?¶

  • The sample sizes are checked against the paper. The test set lengths match to within 13 days (S&P 500) and 8 days ($/£), once the $/£ holidays are handled the way the paper's data evidently handled them.
  • The fitted GARCH model looks like a typical daily GARCH model. In the window shown in Section 2, persistence is 0.997 and the ARCH coefficient is small (0.03).
  • The GPD shape parameter is positive in that window (0.13), which means a heavy but not extreme tail. That's what the residual QQ-plot shows too.
  • The direction of each method's failure has a clear reason. The normal model under-predicts the far tail, so it has too many violations. Unconditional EVT can't react to volatility, so its violations cluster.
  • 19 October 1987 is the single biggest loss in the data, and it violates every method. The 99% forecasts for that day ranged from 2.3% to 4.4%. No model fitted to the previous four years could call a 22.9% fall.

8. Is the procedure right?¶

  • Every forecast uses only the 1,000 losses up to the day before. The forecast for day t + 1 is compared with the loss on day t + 1. Nothing from the future enters the fit.
  • The models are refitted every day, as in the paper. That's the expensive part, and the paper is explicit that it's what they did.
  • The paper's choices are followed exactly: AR(1) mean with no constant, GARCH(1,1), normal pseudo-maximum likelihood for the EVT and normal methods, k = 100 tail points, and a two-sided binomial test with 0.05 as the cut-off.
  • The extension isn't tuned. No setting was changed after looking at the 1993–2026 results.
  • The one place I made a judgement is the $/£ holiday handling, and the sample-size check supports it.

Wrapping up¶

The replication holds up. On the two series with free data for the paper's dates, I match the paper's verdict in 23 of 24 cells, and every count is within two binomial standard deviations. The main finding comes through clearly: model the changing volatility and the fat tail of what's left, and you get VaR forecasts that pass the backtest; drop either part and you don't. The out-of-sample run to 2026 supports that, with one caveat: at the 99.5% level on US equities, even the best method only just passes.

What I'd add next:

  • the paper's Section 4 backtest of expected shortfall, which is now the regulatory measure under the Basel FRTB rules;
  • an asymmetric heavy-tailed GARCH, such as a skewed-t, which might fix the conditional t model's out-of-sample problem;
  • the conditional coverage and dynamic-quantile tests from later papers, which combine the count and independence checks into one.
